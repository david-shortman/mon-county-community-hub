# Reaching people: helpers first, then new folks

Most people who need help do not search for a website. They ask someone they trust: a librarian, a pantry volunteer, a nurse, a school coordinator, a pastor, a neighbor, or 211. This page sets out how the hub reaches residents through those helpers, and how it gets people who have never heard of it connected to the groups already doing the work.

It is the source for the [reach epic (#155)](https://github.com/david-shortman/mon-county-community-hub/issues/155). The charter's limits apply to everything here: agents never contact anyone, and anything that talks to an outside group, spends money, prints, or sends texts waits for David.

## What the research says

**Helpers are trusted because they check things first, and outdated listings cost them time.** A Health Management Associates report on community health workers calls them among the most trusted messengers in their communities. They do not just pass information along; they check it and put it in words people can use. Their biggest drag is scattered, outdated resources. One said, "sometimes I'm scanning the internet and the information is not up-to-date." The report recommends resource verification, shared distribution of checked information, and two-way feedback.

**Local groups value a directory that stays current more than a new platform.** In a study of community organizations offered a referral platform, staff valued the searchable, up-to-date directory. Electronic referrals mattered less, and groups with their own routines and stretched staff did not adopt the new tool. Money incentives did not change that.

**Many people would rather help themselves first, but need an easy person to turn to.** In a randomized study of adults with diabetes who had unmet social needs, 77% of those offered guided online self-navigation used it, against 14% of those offered in-person help. Barriers included stigma, thinking they would not qualify, and trouble with technology. A 2026 study of health workers, doctors, and community groups agreed that self-help tools widen reach, but said hands-on help must stay available, and a bad self-help experience can spread wrong information.

**A warm handoff means telling people what happens next.** Referral network guidance says to tell people who will contact them, when, and how, to reach out quickly, and that texting often works better than calls or email.

**Libraries are trusted places to start.** In a 2017 Pew survey, 78% of U.S. adults said they trust libraries and librarians for information. Library navigator programs elsewhere show what this can look like: a county library system in North Carolina put social workers in all seven branches, saw about two new people a day, and works with about 120 local partners. A regional digital navigator program in New York trained navigators at 45 library and partner sites and helped more than 620 residents.

**Texts reach people; QR codes alone do not.** City trials found texts drew twice the sign-ups of postcards at a fifth of the cost, and a text about rental aid made people 19% more likely to be connected to help. In a clinic study, QR code flyers in 59 rooms and work areas led to about 20 uses, and the authors said codes need an in-person prompt.

**In rural Appalachia, asking for help can feel like failing.** Research with low-income women in Appalachian Kentucky found a strong value on handling things yourself, and shame about asking, kept people from getting help until things were bad. Rural communication guidance says to use local trusted messengers, mix channels (including radio, newspapers, and events), be plain and warm, never judge, and not ignore pressing needs like food and housing.

## Principles

1. **Reach people through the people they already trust.** Helpers are the front door. The hub's job is to make their work easier and faster, not to replace them or compete for attention.
2. **Fresh, checked, and easy to pass on.** A helper should see when a listing was last checked, and be able to print it, text it, or share it in one tap.
3. **Self-serve first, asking is normal, no shame.** Let people find answers on their own. Offer a person as a normal option, not a last resort. Say plainly who qualifies, so people do not rule themselves out.
4. **Every listing says what happens next.** What to bring, what the first call is like, and who can help you through it.
5. **A person hands it over.** Printed cards and QR codes work when someone hands them over and explains them, not when they sit on a wall.
6. **Build on what exists.** 211, the family resource network, the city's resource efforts, and local directories already do this work. Share data openly in their formats instead of building another silo.
7. **Works on any phone.** Low-end Android phones, weak signal, small data plans, older eyes, and low reading levels are the normal case, not the edge case.

## The eight ideas

Each idea lists the issue that carries it and what needs David's yes. Agents may build anything that does not reach outside the project.

### 1. Make it easy to pass on

Print, "text this to myself," and share on every listing, with the checked date showing. The text option is a plain phone link, so the person's own phone sends it and the hub runs no texting service. Issue #157, building on #39.

**Needs David's yes:** nothing.

### 2. A way for helpers to correct us

A report form that asks "I work here," "I help people find services," or "I used this," puts reports from helpers first, and shows fixes on a public corrections page in counts. Issue #158, building on #39 and #48.

**Needs David's yes:** the no-account reporting channel (already in #48), and any public credit to a named group.

### 3. Share the data, don't compete

Publish listings in the Open Referral format so 211, the family resource network, and city efforts can reuse them. Already planned in #36 and #73.

**Needs David's yes:** any data-sharing talks, already in #23 (United Way family resource network) and #24 (WV 211).

### 4. Show up where helpers already meet

The county's family resource network holds regular coordinating meetings with local agencies. A 5-minute demo and a one-page handout, prepped by Duet, would let David show helpers what the hub does for them. Prep is #161. The decision is #163.

**Needs David's yes:** attending, presenting, or sharing the handout.

### 5. Make the library a front door

Library and agency computers should get a clean print view and the same pass-on tools as phones. Whether to ask the library to use the hub or keep cards at the desk is David's call. Build is part of #157. The decision is #163.

**Needs David's yes:** any conversation with the library.

### 6. Say what happens next

New optional fields on each service, quoted from the source: what to bring, what the first call or visit is like, the best time if the source says so, and who can help you through it. Issue #159.

**Needs David's yes:** nothing.

### 7. Printed cards, handed over by a person

Printable wallet cards and half-page posters per topic, each with a QR code, a short web address that is easy to say, and "or call or text 211." Building the files is #160. Printing and placing them is #163.

**Needs David's yes:** printing, spending money, and placing cards anywhere.

### 8. An opt-in text line, off until David says so

A text line where people text a word like FOOD and get back places and hours. The charter lets agents build it but keeps switching it on with David, and it needs an outside account and likely money. Already in #99.

**Needs David's yes:** whether to explore it, the account, the cost, and turning it on.

### Wording for help pages

Principle 3 becomes a tone note for the plain language rubric and a review of the help pages. Issue #162.

## What needs David's yes

- Talking to any outside group or person, including helpers, the library, 211, and the family resource network (#163, #23, #24).
- Printing or placing cards and posters, and any spending (#163).
- A text line, its account, and turning it on (#99).

Notes about specific people, conversations, and outreach plans stay in the private Notion home, never in this repo.

## How we will know it is working

These map to the charter's scoreboard levels.

- **Findability:** helper questions added to the findability test, such as "A family needs food tonight. What do I hand them?"
- **Use:** aggregate counts of print, text, and share clicks, once analytics are decided (#41).
- **Action:** partner-reported numbers only through David, per the measurement playbook.
- **Recognition:** corrections from helpers, and local groups linking to or using the hub.

The reach check (#112) sizes which residents we reach and miss, using public, aggregate data only.
