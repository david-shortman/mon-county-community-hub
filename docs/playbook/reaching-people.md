# Reaching people: through trusted helpers, and directly on their own terms

People find help in two ways. Many ask someone they trust: a librarian, a pantry volunteer, a nurse, a school coordinator, a pastor, a neighbor, or 211. Many others never will. They may feel ashamed to ask, may not want anyone to know, may not live near a helper, or may realize they need help at 11 at night or on a Sunday, when every office is closed.

The hub serves both. It makes trusted helpers' work easier, and it lets anyone find help on their own, at any hour, with no name and no judgment. We also stay open to new ways of sharing information that are not happening anywhere today.

This page is the source for the [reach epic (#155)](https://github.com/david-shortman/mon-county-community-hub/issues/155). The charter's limits apply to everything here: agents never contact anyone, and anything that talks to an outside group, spends money, prints, or sends texts waits for David. Every idea looks for a low or no cost way first and says what it would cost.

## What the research says

### Helpers

**Helpers are trusted because they check things first, and outdated listings cost them time.** A Health Management Associates report on community health workers calls them among the most trusted messengers in their communities. They do not just pass information along; they check it and put it in words people can use. Their biggest drag is scattered, outdated resources. One said, "sometimes I'm scanning the internet and the information is not up-to-date." The report recommends resource verification, shared distribution of checked information, and two-way feedback.

**Local groups value a directory that stays current more than a new platform.** In a study of community organizations offered a referral platform, staff valued the searchable, up-to-date directory. Electronic referrals mattered less, and groups with their own routines and stretched staff did not adopt the new tool. Money incentives did not change that.

**A warm handoff means telling people what happens next.** Referral network guidance says to tell people who will contact them, when, and how, to reach out quickly, and that texting often works better than calls or email.

**Libraries are trusted places to start.** In a 2017 Pew survey, 78% of U.S. adults said they trust libraries and librarians for information. Library navigator programs elsewhere show what this can look like: a county library system in North Carolina put social workers in all seven branches, saw about two new people a day, and works with about 120 local partners. A regional digital navigator program in New York trained navigators at 45 library and partner sites and helped more than 620 residents.

### People on their own

**Many people would rather help themselves first.** In a randomized study of adults with diabetes who had unmet social needs, 77% of those offered guided online self-navigation used it, against 14% of those offered in-person help. Barriers included stigma, thinking they would not qualify, and trouble with technology. A 2026 study of health workers, doctors, and community groups agreed that self-help tools widen reach, but said hands-on help must stay available, and a bad self-help experience can spread wrong information.

**Online screeners lead to real applications.** In a study of an online benefits screener for older adults, 36% of surveyed users applied for at least one benefit and about one in five enrolled.

**Need does not keep office hours.** One county 211 reported that 21% of its contacts came in outside business hours, on weekends or between 5 p.m. and 8 a.m. Most helpers, offices, and pantries are open only part of the week.

**Stigma follows people even when no one is watching.** In a study of 370 college students, those with high self-stigma were far less likely to click a link for mental health information, even in a private online setting (about 2% against about 9% overall). Making help private is not enough. The words have to make asking feel normal.

**In rural Appalachia, asking for help can feel like failing.** Research with low-income women in Appalachian Kentucky found a strong value on handling things yourself, and shame about asking, kept people from getting help until things were bad. The authors suggest working with values of strength and endurance, not against them.

**Phones, not computers, and no accounts.** Code for America's benefits field guide notes that about 27% of Americans earning under $30,000 a year rely on a smartphone for internet, and recommends letting people start without an account. See [audience facts](audience-facts.md) for Mon County numbers.

### Getting the word out

**Texts reach people; QR codes alone do not.** City trials found texts drew twice the sign-ups of postcards at a fifth of the cost, and a text about rental aid made people 19% more likely to be connected to help. In a clinic study, QR code flyers in 59 rooms and work areas led to about 20 uses, and the authors said codes need an in-person prompt.

**Use local messengers and many channels.** Rural communication guidance says to use local trusted messengers, mix channels (including radio, newspapers, and events), be plain and warm, never judge, and not ignore pressing needs like food and housing.

## Principles

1. **Two front doors.** Reach people through the helpers they trust, and directly, on their own terms. Neither replaces the other.
2. **Make helpers faster, not busier.** The hub's job with helpers is to save them time, not to replace them or compete for attention.
3. **Fresh, checked, and easy to pass on.** Anyone should see when a listing was last checked, and be able to print it, text it, or share it in one tap.
4. **Open any hour, no name needed, no judgment.** Everything works at 2 a.m. on a Sunday without an account. We never ask who someone is. Words make asking feel normal, and say plainly who qualifies, so people do not rule themselves out.
5. **Every listing says what happens next.** What to bring, what the first call is like, and who can help you through it, offered as a normal choice, not a last resort.
6. **A person hands it over.** Printed cards and QR codes work when someone hands them over and explains them, not when they sit on a wall.
7. **Try what is not happening yet.** Stay open to new ways of sharing information and helping people talk to each other that no one here offers today, within the charter's limits.
8. **Build on what exists.** 211, the family resource network, the city's resource efforts, and local directories already do this work. Share data openly in their formats instead of building another silo.
9. **Works on any phone, at low or no cost.** Low-end phones, weak signal, small data plans, older eyes, and low reading levels are the normal case. Choose free or low cost tools first and name the cost of every option.

## The ideas

Each idea lists the issue that carries it, what it costs, and what needs David's yes. Agents may build anything that does not reach outside the project.

### Through helpers

#### 1. Make it easy to pass on

Print, "text this to myself," and share on every listing, with the checked date showing. The text option is a plain phone link, so the person's own phone sends it and the hub runs no texting service. Issue #157, building on #39.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** nothing.

#### 2. A way for helpers to correct us

A report form that asks "I work here," "I help people find services," or "I used this," puts reports from helpers first, and shows fixes on a public corrections page in counts. Issue #158, building on #39 and #48.

**Cost:** free if #48 picks a no-cost channel. **Needs David's yes:** the no-account reporting channel (#48), and any public credit to a named group.

#### 3. Share the data, don't compete

Publish listings in the Open Referral format so 211, the family resource network, and city efforts can reuse them. Already planned in #36 and #73.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** any data-sharing talks, already in #23 (United Way family resource network) and #24 (WV 211).

#### 4. Show up where helpers already meet

The county's family resource network holds regular coordinating meetings with local agencies. A 5-minute demo and a one-page handout, prepped by Duet, would let David show helpers what the hub does for them. Prep is #161. The decision is #163.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** attending, presenting, or sharing the handout.

#### 5. Make the library a front door

Library and agency computers should get a clean print view and the same pass-on tools as phones. Whether to ask the library to use the hub or keep cards at the desk is David's call. Build is part of #157. The decision is #163.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** any conversation with the library.

#### 6. Say what happens next

New optional fields on each service, quoted from the source: what to bring, what the first call or visit is like, the best time if the source says so, and who can help you through it. Issue #159.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** nothing.

#### 7. Printed cards, handed over by a person

Printable wallet cards and half-page posters per topic, each with a QR code, a short web address that is easy to say, and "or call or text 211." Building the files is #160. Printing and placing them is #163.

**Cost:** the files are free; printing costs money. **Needs David's yes:** printing, spending, and placing cards anywhere.

#### 8. An opt-in text line, off until David says so

A text line where people text a word like FOOD and get back places and hours. The charter lets agents build it but keeps switching it on with David, and it needs an outside account. Already in #99.

**Cost:** a texting service usually charges per message; nonprofit programs may lower that. **Needs David's yes:** whether to explore it, the account, the cost, and turning it on.

### Directly, on people's own terms

#### 9. A "what help might I get?" path, open any hour

A few plain questions, each on its own small page, ending at programs and places that may fit, with who to call and what to bring. Eligibility is quoted from each source, and the page says "you may qualify," never yes or no. No account, nothing saved on a server. Issue #188, using the question path block from #178.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** nothing.

#### 10. Ask anything, no name needed

A small question box on help pages. No name, no email, no account. Anything that mentions danger shows 911, 988, and 211 on screen right away. Agents answer by improving a public answer page, never by replying to a person or publishing their words. Issue #189.

**Cost:** free with the no-cost channel picked in #48. **Needs David's yes:** the channel (#48).

#### 11. Show how someone like me did it

Short walkthroughs written as clearly labeled made-up examples ("A parent in Westover with a past-due power bill could..."), using real, sourced programs. They make getting help look like a normal, capable step. Issue #190.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** any real person's story, even with consent.

#### 12. Show up where people already are online

Be the answer that search engines and AI assistants give for local questions (#88, #72, #75), and offer a copy-and-share kit on each guide that local groups can paste into their own pages, newsletters, or Facebook groups. Agents never post outside the repo. Issue #191.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** telling any group about the kit.

#### 13. After hours: what you can do right now

When the phone's clock shows most places are closed, show what is open now, what you can do tonight, and what to do first thing on the next open day. Builds on the open now badge in #176. Issue #192.

**Cost:** free. **Needs David's yes:** nothing.

### Wording for help pages

Principle 4 becomes a tone note for the plain language rubric and a review of the help pages. Issue #162.

## How we will know it is working

These map to the charter's scoreboard levels.

- **Findability:** helper questions and after-hours questions added to the findability test, such as "A family needs food tonight. What do I hand them?" and "It is Sunday night and my power is being shut off. What can I do?"
- **Use:** aggregate counts of print, text, and share clicks, question paths finished, and visits outside business hours, once analytics are decided (#41).
- **Action:** partner-reported numbers only through David, per the measurement playbook.
- **Recognition:** corrections from helpers, questions asked, and local groups linking to or sharing the hub.

The reach check (#112) and [audience facts](audience-facts.md) size which residents we reach and miss, using public, aggregate data only.
