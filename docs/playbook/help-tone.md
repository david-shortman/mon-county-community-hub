# Tone for help pages: self-serve first, asking is normal, no shame

How we word any page that helps someone get help: health care, rides, bills, shots, disability, jobs, and anything else where a reader might feel unsure about asking. It turns principle 5 of [reaching people](reaching-people.md#principles) into rules a writer or reviewer can check. It sits on top of the [plain language rubric](../rubrics/plain-language.md) and the [writing rules](writing-rules.md). From #162, part of #155.

## Why tone matters here

- **Shame keeps people from asking, even in private.** In a study of 370 college students, those with high self-stigma were far less likely to click a link for mental health information, even online and alone. Making a page private is not enough; the words have to make asking feel normal. ([reaching people](reaching-people.md#people-on-their-own), citing Lannin and others, 2016)
- **In rural Appalachia, a strong value on handling things yourself can delay help.** Research with low-income women in Appalachian Kentucky found that this value, and shame about asking, kept people from getting help until things were bad. The authors suggest working with values of strength and endurance, not against them. (Snell-Rood and others, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5173451)
- **Many people would rather help themselves first.** In one study, 77% of people offered guided online self-help used it, against 14% of those offered in-person help. ([reaching people](reaching-people.md#people-on-their-own))
- **Rural guidance says be plain and warm, and never judge.** (CDC Foundation, https://www.cdcfoundation.org/communications-guidance-rural-populations)
- **People rule themselves out.** Medicare's own page on Medicare Savings Programs tells people to apply even if they don't think they qualify (https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/help/medicare-savings-programs, quoted on our [Turning 65 page](../../site/src/pages/turning-65.astro), checked October 30, 2026).

## The five rules

### 1. Lead with what the reader can do now, on their own

The first sentence names the thing the reader can do right away without talking to anyone: look something up on the page, read who a program is for, apply online, or check a list. A phone number can be in the first sentence too, but if the page has a self-serve tool, the tool comes first.

**Check:** read only the lede. Is there something the reader can do in the next minute, alone, at night?

### 2. Offer a person as a normal choice, not a last resort

When we point to a person or a phone line, say it the way you would say "the library has free Wi-Fi": a normal thing many people use. Avoid "if all else fails," "as a last resort," or "if you can't figure it out."

Only say something like "lots of people call for this" when a source supports it. If not, use plain facts that make it feel normal: "It is free," "You can call yourself, no referral needed" (only when the source says so), "They help with forms."

**Check:** search the page for "last resort," "if all else fails," "if you can't," "if you need help understanding."

### 3. Say who qualifies, plainly and from the source

Never write "if they qualify" or "for those who are eligible" without saying who that is, on the same screen. Use the source's own rules in plain words. Name the groups people might not know are included (for example, "and people with Medicaid"). We never tell anyone they qualify; the program decides ([About page](../../site/src/pages/about.astro)).

**Check:** search for "qualify," "eligible," "eligibility." Each one has the rule next to it, or a link that jumps straight to it.

### 4. Describe the situation, not the person

Write "No insurance?" or "If money is tight," not "low-income people" or "the needy." Put the program first and the reader's situation second. Income limits are facts and belong in the who-it-is-for line, quoted from the source.

**Never use:** needy, the poor, less fortunate, underprivileged, handout, charity case, deserving. **Use with care, and only quoting a source:** vulnerable, at risk, low-income (as a label for a person). These match the "no pity words" check in [writing rules](writing-rules.md#2-no-deficit-or-pity-framing).

**Check:** search the changed text for the words above.

### 5. Capable, not rescued

The reader is the one taking a smart, normal step. The local group is the one doing the work. The site is neither. Avoid "we're here for you," "don't be ashamed," "don't be afraid to ask." Telling people not to feel shame reminds them of shame. Just make the next step clear.

**Check:** look for sentences about feelings ("don't worry," "no shame," "it's okay to") and replace them with the next step.

## Before and after, from our own pages

These are real ledes and lines from the site as of October 31, 2026. The first three "after" versions were applied to the pages in a follow-up PR for #162. The last group are good examples to copy.

**Self-serve first: Trash, recycling and drop-off** (`/recycling/`)

- Before: "To learn your pickup day, call the Monongalia County Solid Waste Authority at 304-292-3801. We could not find a public map of pickup days."
- Problem: the page opens with a phone call, and a gap, while it has an item lookup right below that answers most questions with no call at all.
- After: "Look up any item below to see where it goes. To learn your pickup day, call the Monongalia County Solid Waste Authority at 304-292-3801."

**Say who qualifies: Flu and COVID shots** (`/flu-shots/`)

- Before: "Children: free through Vaccines for Children if they qualify (see above)."
- Problem: "if they qualify" with no rule, so a parent may assume their child does not.
- After: "Children 18 and younger: free through Vaccines for Children if they have no insurance, insurance that does not cover shots, WV Medicaid or WVCHIP, or are American Indian or Alaska Native." (from the health department's shots page, checked October 29, 2026)

**Describe the situation, not the person: Diabetes, blood pressure or weight** (`/diabetes-and-weight/`)

- Before: "Low income and no insurance? Health Right's free clinic holds diabetes and heart clinics for its patients."
- Problem: it leads with a label, and leaves out that people with Medicaid can use the clinic too.
- After: "No insurance, or not enough? Health Right is a free clinic for West Virginians with low income, and it also takes Medicaid. Its patients can join its diabetes and heart clinics." (from Health Right's own pages, checked October 30, 2026)

**Good examples to copy**

- Turning 65: "Medicare says to apply even if you don't think you qualify." It names the worry and answers it with a source.
- Families and kids: "You do not need a doctor to send you." It removes a barrier people assume.
- Find a job: "Here is where to look, and where to get free help with a resume, interviews or a computer." Self-serve first, then a person, as an equal choice.

Any page a resident reads to get help. Pages in the Help now and Get through this month doors, and pages about housing, money, food or crisis lines, are high stakes: tone fixes there are welcome but the PR waits for David (see "When a PR waits for David" in `docs/COORDINATION.md`). Start with the other pages.

## How to review a page

1. Read the lede out loud. Does it start with something the reader can do alone (rule 1)?
2. Search for: qualify, eligible, last resort, if you can't, needy, low-income, don't worry, no shame.
3. Fix each hit with the matching rule, using the source's words.
4. Keep the page at about an 8th grade level ([plain language rubric](../rubrics/plain-language.md)).
