# Sources with their own slant

Our neutrality rules cover our own words. This page covers sources that have their own interest in the answer: an advocacy group describing a policy, a business describing its own service, a campaign's page, a union, a trade group, a church or a political party. It answers #1095.

Cost: $0. It is a writing rule and a rubric item.

## The rule

**Use a source with its own interest for facts about itself. Use an official or neutral source for anything about other people, rights, rules or policy.**

| The source says | Use it? |
|---|---|
| Its own hours, address, phone, what it offers, what it costs, who it serves | Yes. It is the owner of those facts (tier 1 in the [source order](source-order.md)). |
| Its own events and how to join | Yes, as for any group. |
| What a law, rule or benefit says ("you have the right to...", "you qualify if...") | No. Use the office that runs it or the law itself: the state code, the agency's own page or form. |
| What a policy will do, or whether it is good or bad | No. We do not say this at all (charter: neutrality). |
| Facts about another group, a rival business or an opponent | No. Use that group's own page. |
| Numbers about the community (how many people, how much money) | Only with the original data source it cites, read by us. Cite the data, not the group. |

## How to apply it

1. **Ask who gains.** Before using a source, ask: does this group gain if a reader believes this? If yes, use it only for facts about itself.
2. **Find the owner of the rule.** For rights, benefits and rules, go to the office that runs the program or the text of the rule. An advocacy group's plain explainer can help you find the right law or form, the way a directory helps you find a group. Then read the law or form.
3. **Describe, don't repeat the pitch.** A business's own page is a fine source for its hours. Its claims about itself ("the best", "the only", "trusted") stay out ([trust rubric TR3](../rubrics/trust-and-civic-ethics.md#tr3-neutrality)).
4. **When official and interested sources differ,** the official one sets what we state. If two official or neutral sources differ, follow [say who says what](../decisions/2026-10-29-1300-when-sources-differ.md).
5. **Candidates and campaigns:** never a source for anything but their own filing status, and that comes from the WV Secretary of State or the county clerk, not the campaign.

## Examples

- **A legal aid group's page explains tenant rights.** Use it for the legal aid group's own intake hours and phone. For what the law says about a landlord's notice, cite the West Virginia Code section, and point readers to the legal aid group for help with their own case.
- **A utility's page describes its payment assistance.** That is the utility's own program, so its page is the owner of the program's rules. A consumer group's page about the same program can help you find it, but is not the source.
- **A group's page says "1 in 4 children here go hungry."** Find and read the data it cites (for example the food bank's or a federal survey). Cite that data with its year, or leave the number out.

## How it is checked

- **Writers:** the [source order](source-order.md) and this page, for every new or changed fact.
- **The PR review:** [TR10 in the trust rubric](../rubrics/trust-and-civic-ethics.md#tr10-sources-with-their-own-slant) asks the reviewer to flag a claim about rights, benefits, rules or other groups that rests only on an interested source.
- **The second check** ([trust and AI](trust-and-ai.md#the-independent-second-check), step 2) already sends the checker to the program's own page for top-tier items.
